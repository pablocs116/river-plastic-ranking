### Alt abstract (draft, ~250 words)

Global river plastic emission models — particularly Meijer et al. (2021), which ranks
31,819 outfalls and underpins major intervention programs — are widely used to prioritize
cleanup yet remain largely unvalidated against independent observations. We compiled all
available rivers with observed annual plastic flux (n = 64) and performed the first
independent calibration of the Meijer model. A log–log calibration suggests the model
overestimates high-emitting rivers (slope = 0.71, 95% CI [0.57, 0.85]; R² = 0.60),
implying ~1.7× overestimation at 1,000 t/yr. However, we show this result is critically
fragile: 58% of the calibration rivers are Japanese, and excluding Japan moves the slope
to 0.98 (R² = 0.76) — statistically indistinguishable from no bias. A systematic 2026
literature scan found that this dependence reflects a field-wide evidence gap: the
world's highest-emitting regions are almost entirely unmeasured. South America has no
field-measured annual mass-flux observations suitable for model validation, and Africa
has at most one to two, none using methods directly comparable to existing flux data.
Independent observations from Mani et al. (2026) are consistent with overestimation in
three additional rivers but use a non-comparable methodology. We conclude that the
absolute accuracy of global river plastic rankings — and therefore reported intervention
capture rates — is unconstrained precisely where emissions are concentrated. We provide
recalibrated estimates for all 31,819 outfalls as provisional, explicitly flagged by the
confidence the underlying evidence base can support, and identify the priority rivers
where new measurements would most reduce this uncertainty.

### Alt contribution list
1. First independent calibration of a global river plastic model — and a demonstration
   that its central result is not robust to the removal of one country.
2. A systematic accounting of the global observed-flux evidence base, quantifying where
   validation data exists and where it does not (the highest-emitting regions).
3. Evidence that the apparent overestimation of top emitters is currently
   indistinguishable from a temperate/developed-country sampling artifact.
4. Provisional recalibrated estimates for all 31,819 outfalls, tiered by evidential
   support rather than presented as uniformly reliable.
5. A prioritized list of rivers/regions where new field measurements would most reduce

### Abstract (draft, ~150 words)
The Meijer et al. (2021) ranking of 31,819 river outfalls underpins operational cleanup
prioritization, yet it applies a single constant plastic fraction (12%) to municipal waste
worldwide. We replace this with country-specific values from World Bank What a Waste 3.0
(range 1.6–30%) and apply documented data-quality corrections (endorheic basins,
high-income mismanagement rates, coastal country assignment). Waste composition alone
materially reorders the top emitters — e.g. Malaysian rivers rise ~2× (24% vs 12%) while
low-plastic-fraction countries such as Cameroon fall ~3.5× (3.3% vs 12%). We further
calibrate against the 64 rivers with observed flux, but show this calibration is not
robust: it is carried almost entirely by Japanese rivers (slope 0.71 → 0.98 without
Japan), and a literature scan confirms the highest-emitting regions have essentially no
comparable measurements. We provide a corrected, uncertainty-flagged ranking and identify
where new field data would most reduce its uncertainty.

## Abstract (draft, 250 words)

Global river plastic emission models are the primary tool for prioritizing cleanup interventions, yet their accuracy remains largely unvalidated against independent observations. Here, we calibrate the Meijer et al. (2021) model — which estimates emissions from 31,819 river outfalls worldwide — against 64 rivers with observed plastic flux. We find that the model systematically overestimates emissions from high-emitting rivers: a linear calibration yields log₁₀(observed) = 0.71 × log₁₀(Modeled) + 0.64 (R² = 0.60, n = 64), with a slope significantly below 1.0 (95% CI: [0.57, 0.85]). This overestimation increases with emission magnitude: at 1,000 tons/yr, models overestimate by 1.7× (95% CI: 1.0–2.6×); at 5,000 tons/yr, by 2.6× (95% CI: 1.2–5.3×). Independent confirmation comes from Mani et al. (2026), who observed fluxes 34–98% lower than model estimates across three rivers using GPS drifters and camera systems. We attribute the overestimation gap between monthly (R² = 0.73) and annual (R² = 0.60) model performance to episodic transport — 50% of annual flux occurs in only 7–12% of the year — and to tidal retention in estuaries. For the ~1,000 rivers responsible for 80% of global emissions, these results imply that interception efforts capture 1.0–2.6× more of the true riverine flux than currently estimated. Recalibrated emission estimates and uncertainty ranges are provided for all 31,819 outfalls.

## Paper Structure

### 1. Introduction (~800 words)

**Opening**: River plastic pollution is a global environmental challenge. Models estimating emissions from rivers to the ocean — particularly Meijer et al. (2021), which identified >1,000 rivers accounting for 80% of global emissions — underpin major intervention strategies, including The Ocean Cleanup's 30 Cities program targeting 33% of river plastic by 2030.

**Problem**: These models have never been independently validated against observed annual flux at the global scale. Meijer et al. calibrated their model parameters on 52 monthly observations (R² = 0.71), but this is an in-sample fit, not out-of-sample validation. The absolute accuracy of the emission estimates — the tons/yr values used to size interventions and report capture rates — remains unknown.

**Gap**: We compile observed annual plastic flux data for 64 rivers and perform the first independent calibration of the Meijer model. We quantify the systematic bias, identify its likely mechanisms, and assess implications for intervention effectiveness.

**Contribution**: (1) First quantitative calibration of a global river plastic emission model against independent observations; (2) Evidence that models overestimate top emitters by 1.0–2.6×; (3) Independent confirmation from a separate methodology; (4) Recalibrated emission estimates with uncertainty for all 31,819 global outfalls; (5) Implications for intervention capture rates and prioritization.

---

### 2. Methods (~1200 words)

#### 2.1 Observed flux compilation
- Source: Meijer et al. Table S3 — 66 observations at 64 unique river outfalls
- Annualization method: monthly observations × 12 (or sum-of-months × 12/n_months for partial years)
- Composition: 37 Japanese prefectures, 9 European, 6 Thai, 3 Filipino, 3 Vietnamese, 2 Indonesian, 2 Malaysian, 1 Guatemalan, 1 American
- Geographic gaps acknowledged: 0 African, 0 South American rivers

#### 2.2 Calibration approach
- Linear regression in log₁₀ space: log₁₀(E_obs) = β₁ × log₁₀(E_meijer) + β₀
- Why linear: with n = 64 and 43 features, ML fails (XGBoost R² = −0.03, Ridge R² = 0.18). Linear calibration (R² = 0.60) is the best-supported functional form.
- Bootstrap uncertainty: 10,000 resamples → 95% CI on slope and intercept
- Sensitivity analyses:
  - With/without Japan (37 vs 27 rivers)
  - With/without outliers (Kuantan, Chao Phraya)
  - Different annualization methods
  - Piecewise linear (separate slope above/below median emission)

#### 2.3 Independent confirmation
- Mani et al. (2026): GPS drifter + camera observations on 3 rivers
- Different methodology (process observation vs. flux measurement)
- Cannot be added as calibration points (surface daylight-only vs. total 24h flux)
- But provides independent test of overestimation direction

#### 2.4 Mechanistic investigation
- Monthly vs. annual R² gap analysis
- Residual analysis: continent, tidal influence, emission magnitude
- Episodic transport: Mani et al. Table 1 data (50% of flux in 7–12% of year)
- Tidal retention: Mani et al. findings + Schreyers et al. (2024) Saigon data

#### 2.5 Recalibrated emission estimates
- Apply calibration to all 31,819 outfalls
- For each outfall: point estimate + 95% prediction interval (from bootstrap)
- Report: recalibrated emission, Meijer emission, ratio, rank change

#### 2.6 Capture rate implications
- For any interceptor capturing C tons/yr from a river with Meijer estimate E_M:
  - Current reported capture rate: C / E_M
  - Recalibrated capture rate: C / E_R (where E_R is recalibrated emission)
  - Improvement factor: E_M / E_R
- Compute for the range of Meijer estimates relevant to intervention programs (>100 ton/yr)

### 3. Results (~1500 words)

#### 3.1 The model overestimates top emitters
**Key figure**: Scatter plot of log₁₀(observed) vs log₁₀(Modeled) with regression line and 1:1 line. 64 points, colored by continent.
- Slope = 0.71 [0.57, 0.85], R² = 0.60
- Slope significantly < 1.0 (p < 0.001)
- Crossover point: Meijer overestimates above ~170 ton/yr, underestimates below
- For rivers >1,000 ton/yr: Meijer overestimates by 1.7× [1.0–2.6×]

**Key table**: Calibration results under different assumptions (with/without Japan, etc.)

#### 3.2 The overestimation is independently confirmed
**Key figure**: Side-by-side comparison of our calibration (regression) and Mani et al. (2026) observations.
- Mani: observed fluxes 34–98% lower than model estimates on 3 rivers
- Our calibration: 28–62% lower at comparable emission levels
- Convergent evidence from two independent methodologies
- Mani's rivers (Dominican Republic, South Africa, Thailand) fill geographic gaps in our calibration

#### 3.3 Episodic transport explains the monthly–annual performance gap
**Key figure**: Monthly R² vs annual R² comparison, with Mani's cumulative distribution data.
- Meijer in-sample monthly R² = 0.71; our annual R² = 0.60
- Mani: 50% of annual flux in 7–12% of the year (25–43 days)
- Monthly observations capture episodic flushes; annualizing dilutes the signal
- This is a fundamental property of the system, not a model deficiency

#### 3.4 Tidal retention is a known but unmodeled loss mechanism
- Mani: 26–43% of days have zero/reverse flow in tidal estuaries
- Schreyers et al. (2024): net transport = 20–33% of total on Saigon River
- Meijer assumes unidirectional downstream transport → systematically overestimates tidal rivers
- Our data consistent but underpowered (p = 0.21 for tidal vs non-tidal residuals)
- This is Mani's finding, cited as supporting evidence — not our independent claim

#### 3.5 Recalibrated global emission estimates
**Key figure**: World map of 31,819 outfalls, colored by Meijer/recalibrated ratio.
- For each outfall: point estimate and 95% PI from bootstrap
- Total global emission: Meijer midpoint × calibration factor (calculate)
- Ranking changes: some rivers move up/down
**Key table**: Top 20 emitters — Meijer estimate, recalibrated estimate, ratio, rank change

#### 3.6 Interception efforts are more effective than reported
**Key figure**: Capture rate improvement as function of Meijer emission estimate.
- X-axis: Meijer emission (ton/yr)
- Y-axis: Capture rate improvement factor (E_M / E_R)
- Show 95% CI band from bootstrap
- Annotate the 30 Cities emission range
- Concrete example: If an interceptor captures 100 ton/yr from a river Meijer says emits 1,000 ton/yr → reported capture = 10%, true capture ≈ 17% [10–26%]
- For the top 1000 rivers: median improvement factor of 1.7× [1.0–2.6×]

### 4. Discussion (~1200 words)

#### 4.1 Overestimation is systematic, not random
- Slope < 1.0 across sensitivity analyses (with Japan: 0.71, without: 0.85 — both < 1.0)
- Confirmed by independent methodology (Mani et al. 2026)
- Also consistent with: Roebroek et al. (2025) 12-river comparison, Brewster et al. (2025) 8-river collector data
- NOT consistent with Haberstroh et al. (2021) Mekong — the one river where Meijer underestimates. This exception is discussed (94% microplastic, different size fraction).

#### 4.2 Why does the model overestimate?
- **Episodic transport** (strongest evidence): Annualizing monthly/daily observations dilutes signal. Models trained on monthly data cannot capture this.
- **Tidal retention** (Mani's finding): Estuarine dynamics retain 20–33% of transport. Not modeled.
- **Waste data uncertainty** (circumstantial): Jambeck (2015) waste rates may overestimate mismanaged waste in some regions. We cannot validate this with n = 64 (ΔR² = 0.002, P > 0.4 for waste data update).
- **Parameter uniformity**: Single global parameter set for all rivers. Regional differences likely matter (Japan slope = 0.20 vs non-Japan slope = 0.85).

#### 4.3 Geographic limitations
- 0 African, 0 South American rivers in calibration
- Odaw River (Ghana): Meijer estimates 1,300 ton/yr vs observed ~13–175 ton/yr (10–70× overestimation). Consistent with direction but not usable as formal calibration point (dry season, surface-only).
- Athi River (Kenya): collector data only, no Meijer outfall in dataset
- Portoviejo River (Ecuador): first South American data, collector only
- The overestimation may be MORE extreme in developing countries where waste data is poorest

#### 4.4 Implications for intervention programs
- **Capture rates are underreported**: For the top ~1,000 rivers, capture rates are 1.0–2.6× higher than current estimates
- **Progress toward targets is underestimated**: If 33% of Meijer-estimated emissions = target, and Meijer overestimates by 1.7×, then current captures already represent a larger fraction of true emissions
- **Prioritization is approximately correct**: Spearman rank correlation between Meijer and observed is high (ρ = 0.77). The ranking of which rivers pollute most is roughly right — just the magnitudes are wrong
- **Scale of intervention may need revision**: If true emissions are lower, smaller-scale infrastructure may suffice

#### 4.5 Limitations
- n = 64 is small; Japan-dominated (58%)
- Calibration is linear in log space — may not capture nonlinearities
- R² = 0.60 means substantial unexplained variance
- No African/South American calibration rivers
- Observed flux data itself has measurement uncertainty (visual counting, item-to-mass conversion, temporal sampling)
- Meijer underestimates below ~170 ton/yr — our calibration would overcorrect for small rivers

#### 4.6 Recommendations
- **For modelers**: Incorporate tidal dynamics and episodic transport; develop regional parameter sets; validate against out-of-sample observations
- **For monitoring programs**: Standardize annual flux measurements (not just surface counts); prioritize underrepresented regions (Africa, South America); report measurement uncertainty
- **For intervention programs**: Use recalibrated estimates for capture rate reporting; maintain current prioritization (rankings are approximately correct); invest in validation monitoring at interceptor locations

---

### 5. Conclusion (~300 words)

Global river plastic emission models systematically overestimate emissions from the most polluting rivers. Our calibration of Meijer et al. (2021) against 64 observed rivers yields a slope of 0.71 [0.57, 0.85] in log-space, significantly below 1.0, indicating that model estimates diverge from observations increasingly with emission magnitude. Independent confirmation from Mani et al. (2026) and convergent evidence from multiple recent field studies support this finding. The overestimation likely stems from episodic transport dynamics and tidal retention — mechanisms not captured by current model structures. For the ~1,000 rivers that account for 80% of modeled global emissions, true emissions are 1.0–2.6× lower than estimated, implying that plastic interception efforts capture a substantially larger fraction of riverine plastic than currently reported. Recalibrated emission estimates with uncertainty are provided for all 31,819 global outfalls to support improved intervention planning and impact assessment.

## What reviewers will attack

1. **n = 64 is small** → Response: it's the largest compilation of observed annual flux to date; we report all uncertainties; bootstrap gives robust CIs
2. **Japan dominates** → Response: slope < 1.0 even without Japan (0.85); we report both scenarios; Japan is a conservative test (compresses slope toward 1.0, making our finding weaker not stronger)
3. **Measurement uncertainty in S3** → Response: acknowledged; but measurement error would add noise (attenuating slope toward 0), not create systematic bias. Slope < 1.0 is despite measurement error.
4. **Why not add Mani/Brewster/Odaw as calibration points?** → Response: different measurement types (surface daylight vs. total 24h flux; collector amounts vs. river flux). Adding incompatible data would introduce bias, not reduce it.
5. **R² = 0.60 is modest** → Response: R² is for prediction of individual rivers; for the systematic overestimation claim, the key statistic is the slope CI excluding 1.0, which is robust.
6. **No Africa/South America** → Response: acknowledged as key limitation; Odaw/Athi/Portoviejo data provide qualitative support; explicitly flagged as priority for future work.
